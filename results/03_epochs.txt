--- ERD IN THE CLASSIFIER WINDOW (the physiology check) ---
  mean band-power change vs rest, 0.5-2.5 s, in %:

  imagined left hand                -19.0%            -18.2%
  imagined right hand               -13.3%             -4.1%

  What theory predicts: motor control crosses over, so imagining the
  LEFT hand should drop power more at C4, and the RIGHT hand more at C3.
  Lateralisation index LI = ERD(C3) - ERD(C4), in percentage points:
    left  hand -> LI should be POSITIVE (C4 drops more)
    right hand -> LI should be NEGATIVE (C3 drops more)
    measured: LI(left) = -0.8 pts, LI(right) = -9.2 pts
    lateralisation contrast LI(left) - LI(right) = +8.4 pts
    (should be clearly positive if imagery is lateralised)

  -> Direction matches theory, contrast +8.4 pts is above the
     3-point noise floor we set. Weak but present.
  Either way, CSP does not depend on C3/C4 specifically: it searches all
  64 electrodes for whatever combination separates the classes. What this
  check tells us is whether that combination is likely to be MOTOR.

--- WHERE IS THE BETWEEN-CLASS DIFFERENCE ON THE SCALP? ---
  A classifier does not care about ERD; it cares about any difference
  between the two classes. So the real question is not 'is there ERD?'
  but 'where does the difference live?'. If it lives over the
  sensorimotor strip, we are decoding motor imagery. If it lives
  somewhere else, we are decoding something else.

  sensorimotor                 mean |LEFT-RIGHT| =    7.2 pts (max 18.8)
  posterior (visual/parietal)  mean |LEFT-RIGHT| =   30.2 pts (max 65.7)
  single strongest electrode: O1 (+65.7 pts)

  -> RED FLAG: the between-class difference is 4.2x LARGER
     posteriorly than over the sensorimotor strip. Something other
     than motor imagery separates these two classes. What?

  HYPOTHESIS 1 — it is the visual cue. This dataset cues trials with
  a target on the left or right of the screen, visible for the whole
  trial. Vision crosses over too, so a target on the RIGHT is seen by
  the LEFT visual cortex (O1).
  Prediction: right-hand trials drop more at O1, left-hand at O2.
    right_hand : O1    +2.2%  O2    -1.1%  -> stronger drop at O2 (NOT as predicted)
    left_hand  : O1   +67.9%  O2   +60.7%  -> stronger drop at O2 (as predicted)
  -> REJECTED. The prediction fails, and the posterior effect is
     roughly the same on both sides — it is bilateral, not
     lateralised. A visual response to a one-sided target would
     have to be lateralised. So this is not the cue.

  HYPOTHESIS 2 — a handful of extreme trials. Occipital alpha is the
  biggest rhythm in our band and it explodes when someone blinks a
  lot or briefly drifts off. The mean is not robust to that; the
  median is. If the effect is real, both should show it.
    left_hand  : mean   +34.5%  median   +18.8%  worst trial  +341.4%
    right_hand : mean    +4.3%  median    +0.7%  worst trial   +57.3%
    between-class gap: +30.2 pts on means, +18.0 pts on medians
  -> Partly. One extreme trial inflates the mean, but the gap survives
     the median, so it is not the work of a single bad trial either.

  HYPOTHESIS 3 — trial order. If one class happened to occur later in
  the session, growing drowsiness (which raises alpha) would look
  like a class effect. That requires the class order to be blocked.
    class order in the recording: RLLRRLRLRLLRLRLLRLRLRLRRLLRRLLRLRLLRRLLRRLRLR
    correlation(trial index, posterior power) = -0.14
  -> REJECTED. The two classes alternate irregularly, i.e. the order
     is randomised, and the drift with trial index is weak. A
     randomised order cannot systematically favour one class.

  WHAT WE ARE LEFT WITH. The posterior difference is not the visual
  cue, not one bad trial, and not fatigue. The remaining and most
  likely explanation is the dullest one: sampling noise. Occipital
  alpha varies enormously from trial to trial, and with ~22 trials
  per class a gap of this size can appear by chance. We cannot
  settle it with 45 trials — and saying so is the honest answer.

  WHY IT MATTERS ANYWAY, concretely, for step 5 and 6:
  CSP maximises a variance ratio over all 64 electrodes. It has no
  notion of anatomy and will happily build its filters out of
  high-variance posterior channels if that separates the classes in
  the training data. That is textbook overfitting, and with 45 trials
  for a 64x64 covariance matrix we are squarely in the regime where
  it happens.
  ACTION for step 6: run the pipeline twice — once on all 64
  electrodes, once restricted to the sensorimotor strip. If the
  restricted version does as well or better, the extra electrodes
  were feeding the classifier noise, and we will have measured it
  instead of guessing.
