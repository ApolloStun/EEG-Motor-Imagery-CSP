--- FINDING 3 FIRST: HOW WELL DOES THE METHOD WORK, REALLY? ---
  sensorimotor strip : 59.9 % (median 53.4, range 42.0 - 96.9)
  all 64 electrodes  : 62.8 % (median 56.8, range 43.3 - 98.4)
  mean baseline      : 52.0 %

  READ THE MEDIAN, NOT THE MEAN:
    mean                       59.9 %
    median                     53.4 %
    mean without the top 2      51.4 %
    mean majority baseline     52.0 %
  The median sits barely above the baseline, and with the two best
  subjects removed the pipeline is AT chance. Two people out of ten carry
  the entire average. Quoting the mean alone would describe a decoder
  that does not exist for eight of these ten subjects.

  subjects individually above chance (p < 0.05, motor strip): 3 / 10
  spread between best and worst subject: 54.9 points
  That spread is the calibration argument made concrete: a single shared
  model would have to average over people this different from each other.
  It also matches the known 'BCI illiteracy' figure — 15-30 % of healthy
  subjects produce no decodable motor imagery — which predicts that some
  subjects here should simply not work. That is an expected property of
  the paradigm, not a bug in the pipeline.

--- FINDING 1: WAS SUBJECT 1'S MISSING LEFT-HAND LATERALISATION REAL? ---
  Theory: LI(left) > 0 (C4 drops more), LI(right) < 0 (C3 drops more).
  LI(left)  across subjects: mean -2.5 pts, 2/10 with the predicted sign
  LI(right) across subjects: mean -6.2 pts, 8/10 with the predicted sign
  contrast LI(left)-LI(right): mean +3.7 pts, 7/10 above the 3-pt noise floor

  CAREFUL — our own metric has an offset:
    mean LI across BOTH hands pooled: -4.4 pts
    subjects where C3 drops more than C4 for BOTH hands: 6/10
    So C3 tends to show more ERD than C4 whatever the hand. That is a
    global hemispheric offset, not a hand-specific pattern, and it
    partly manufactures the per-hand counts above: it pushes both LIs
    negative, which 'confirms' the right hand and 'refutes' the left.
    The offset-free measure is the CONTRAST between hands, because a
    constant added to both cancels in the subtraction. That contrast is
    +3.7 pts on average and clears the noise floor in 7/10 subjects,
    so the lateralised DIFFERENCE BETWEEN HANDS is real even though the
    per-hand story is muddier than step 4 suggested.

  -> VERDICT, stated carefully. Subject 1 was not an accident: the
     same asymmetry appears across subjects. But we CANNOT conclude
     'the right hand lateralises and the left does not', because most
     subjects show C3 dropping more than C4 for BOTH hands. A global
     hemispheric offset of that kind reproduces the per-hand counts
     on its own, so the counts are not evidence for the dominant /
     non-dominant story.
     What DOES survive the offset is the hand-to-hand contrast, which
     is positive and above the noise floor in most subjects. So:
     there IS a lateralised difference between imagining one hand and
     the other; whether the left hand is specifically weaker remains
     unresolved, and distinguishing the two would need handedness
     information the dataset does not provide.

--- FINDING 2: IS THE POSTERIOR SIGNAL THE PROTOCOL OR SUBJECT 1? ---
  subjects where the between-class difference is LARGER posteriorly: 6 / 10
  ratio posterior/sensorimotor: median 1.5, range 0.5 - 5.3
  strongest electrode per subject: ['O1', 'FT8', 'PO7', 'F5', 'POz', 'POz', 'T9', 'Fpz', 'PO8', 'Fz']
    posterior: 5/10   sensorimotor: 0/10   elsewhere: 5/10

  Which anatomical family does each subject's strongest electrode fall in?
    posterior (visual / attention)              5/10  S1 S3 S5 S6 S9
    sensorimotor (what we want)                 0/10  
    temporal (jaw / neck muscle, EMG)           2/10  S2 S7
    frontopolar / frontal (eye movement, EOG)   3/10  S4 S8 S10

  NOT ONE subject has its strongest between-class electrode over the
  sensorimotor strip. The peaks are occipital, temporal or frontal —
  and those three families have well-known non-brain sources: eye
  movements frontally, jaw and neck muscle temporally, visual and
  attentional activity posteriorly. The confound family is therefore
  broader than the 'posterior' one step 4 chased.

  -> Mixed: it happens in some subjects and not others. So it is
     neither a protocol artefact nor a fluke of one recording, but a
     per-subject property. Practically that is the worst case: it
     cannot be corrected once for the whole dataset, and it means
     per-subject pattern inspection is not optional.

  CROSS-CHECK — WHO CARRIES THE AVERAGE, AND WHERE IS THEIR SIGNAL?
    S7: 96.9 % motor-strip, strongest electrode T9 <- temporal, an EMG-prone site
    S2: 90.9 % motor-strip, strongest electrode FT8 <- temporal, an EMG-prone site
    Both of the two subjects carrying the entire group average have
    their strongest between-class difference at a temporal electrode.
    Temporal sites pick up jaw and neck muscle activity, which is
    exactly the contamination we filtered 8-30 Hz partly to avoid —
    and EMG extends into the upper part of that band.
    This does NOT prove their results are muscle. Their motor-strip
    accuracy is high on its own, and lateral strip channels (C5, C6,
    FC5, FC6) are near enough to temporal sites that the question
    cannot be settled by electrode position alone. But the two
    strongest results in the study are the two where a non-brain
    explanation is most available, and reporting a group mean without
    saying so would be misleading.
    What would settle it: EMG-specific checks (high-frequency power,
    ICA artifact components), which are outside this project's scope
    and are recorded as the most valuable next diagnostic.
